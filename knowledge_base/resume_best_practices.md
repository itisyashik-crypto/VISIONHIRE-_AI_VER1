# Resume Best Practices

## ATS Optimization
Use a text-readable PDF or DOCX accepted by the employer. Keep a single-column layout where possible, standard section headings, selectable text, and consistent date formats. Avoid tables, text boxes, decorative icons, skill-rating graphics, and important details in headers or footers. Spell out an acronym once and include common equivalents naturally. Never keyword-stuff or claim a tool that was not used.

## Recommended Structure
1. Name and appropriate contact details; omit photo, birth date, marital status, and unrelated personal data.
2. Role-relevant professional summary, customized honestly for the target role.
3. Skills grouped by discipline, with proficiency claims supported elsewhere.
4. Experience in reverse chronological order, with employer, role, dates, and accomplishment bullets.
5. Selected projects with problem, individual contribution, technology, and outcome.
6. Education and relevant certifications.
7. Optional publications, open-source contributions, awards, or community work relevant to the role.

## Professional Summary
Use two to four concise lines covering the candidate's role direction, relevant domain, strongest supported capabilities, and scope of impact. Avoid generic claims such as “hardworking team player.” Example: “Data analyst with experience building SQL reporting pipelines and stakeholder dashboards; recently automated recurring quality checks for a multi-source reporting workflow.” Include only claims the resume can substantiate.

## Skills Section
Group skills (for example, Languages, Data, Cloud, Frameworks, Methods). Prioritize role-relevant skills and use recognizable names. Distinguish hands-on proficiency from familiarity. Avoid unsupported star bars and exhaustive keyword lists. For each priority skill, provide an example in experience or projects.

## Projects Section
For each selected project, explain:
- The user, business, or technical problem.
- The candidate's own role and contribution.
- Design choices, tools, and meaningful constraints.
- Validation, testing, deployment, or collaboration.
- Measurable outcome where a defensible measurement exists.

Example format: “Built a Python and SQL reconciliation workflow for monthly reports; added validation checks and exception output, reducing manual review steps (measurement available on request).” Do not invent metrics or overstate team work as individual ownership.

## Experience Section
Use action and scope, not responsibility lists. State what was delivered, the candidate's contribution, relevant scale, and outcome. Make dates and titles consistent. Explain unusual context only when useful. Do not expose confidential employer or customer information.

## Certifications and Achievements
List exact credential name and issuer, completion or expiry date where relevant, and verification link or ID only when safe. Separate completed credentials from courses in progress. Include achievements that are relevant, verifiable, and contextualized.

## Portfolio Guidelines
A portfolio should make the candidate's contribution and result easy to inspect. Provide a short overview, screenshots or demo where safe, architecture or process notes, limitations, and links to code or writing. Remove secrets, customer data, and employer-confidential material. Explain what was personally built.

## GitHub Guidelines
Pin a small set of relevant, maintained repositories. Provide a useful README, setup instructions, sample data that is licensed and non-sensitive, tests, and clear commit or contribution context. Include notebooks with reproducible environments and conclusions. A public contribution count is not a measure of skill; private work can be described without disclosure.

## Common Resume Mistakes
- Dense layouts, unreadable text, inconsistent dates, or parser-hostile formatting.
- Generic summaries and long lists disconnected from the target role.
- Unsupported claims, invented impact numbers, or unclear individual contribution.
- Repeating duties without evidence of decisions, outcomes, or learning.
- Listing tools without showing how they were used.
- Including sensitive personal data or confidential work details.
- Treating missing keywords as a reason to make false claims.

## Resume Review Framework
Review in this order: readability and parsing; role relevance; factual clarity; capability evidence; scope and outcomes; dates and consistency; project/portfolio links; privacy and confidentiality. Record uncertainty as a follow-up question, not a negative assumption.

## Resume Scoring Criteria
A role-specific review can use these dimensions, each rated 1–5 only when evidence is present:

| Dimension | Weight | Strong evidence |
|---|---:|---|
| Role-relevant skills | 30% | Skills demonstrated in work or projects |
| Experience and scope | 20% | Clear ownership and relevant complexity |
| Project quality | 20% | Problem, contribution, method, validation, outcome |
| Clarity and completeness | 15% | Readable, consistent, verifiable presentation |
| Growth evidence | 15% | Learning, feedback, increased responsibility |

Weights must be tailored and consistently used. “Not observed” is not zero. This framework is advisory and must not replace interviews or human review.

## Recruiter Insights
A strong resume makes job-relevant evidence easy to find; it is not necessarily the most polished document. Consider equivalent evidence from portfolios, work samples, and interviews. Offer candidates a fair route to clarify parser errors or omitted context.

## Examples
**Weak:** “Responsible for dashboards.”

**Stronger:** “Developed a Power BI dashboard from SQL reporting tables, documented metric definitions, and reviewed the result with finance stakeholders.”

**Weak:** “Expert in cloud.”

**Stronger:** “Deployed a containerized API to AWS and documented monitoring, access control, and rollback steps.”
